Course Description

This course has two goals. The first is to introduce you to central problems in the historical and philosophical study of Zen: transmission and lineage, sudden awakening, no-thought and ordinary mind, encounter dialogue and kōan practice, Song meditation debates, Dōgen and practice-realization, Rinzai training, ritual institutions, gender and ordination, nationalism, and Buddhist modernism. The second is to teach you how to work on those problems yourself: reconstruct a claim precisely, distinguish kinds of evidence, identify the strongest rival explanation, and revise your view when a better account explains more of the record.

Requirements

Readings

Complete the readings before the session for which they are assigned. The list deliberately pairs primary texts with modern historical scholarship where the provenance or later reception of the primary source is part of the problem.

Attendance: Lectures

The course is designed around 25 one-hour lecture sessions. In a self-guided use of this edition, work through the corresponding lecture handout in order and keep written answers to its explicit questions, cases, objections, and argument reconstructions.

Attendance and Participation: Recitation Sections

Recitation is for reconstructing arguments, testing source claims, and giving objections in a form the other side would accept. Participation should make the discussion more exact, not merely add personal reactions.

Presentations

Give one short presentation on a topic chosen from the readings. State one historical or philosophical claim, the evidence for it, the strongest objection, and what would change your mind.

Papers

You will write three 1,000-to-1,200 word papers and one 2,000-to-2,400 word final paper, for a minimum of 5,000 words. You will revise and resubmit Paper 1 after feedback. A strong paper reconstructs the view under discussion accurately before criticizing it, distinguishes historical from normative claims, and explains what its evidence can and cannot establish.

Plagiarism

All writing must be your own. Quote exact wording with a citation, and cite distinctive ideas even when paraphrased. Do not silently treat a late Zen source as a transcript of the earlier master it portrays. If a claim depends on a translation, textual stratum, institutional history, or another scholar's reconstruction, make that dependence explicit.
